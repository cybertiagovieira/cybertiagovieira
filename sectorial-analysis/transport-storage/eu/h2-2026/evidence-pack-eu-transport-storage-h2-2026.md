# Evidence Pack - EU Transport & Storage Cyber-Risk Analysis

**Report date:** 15 September 2026  
**Analysis period:** H2 2026 year to date, through the evidence cut-off  
**Geography:** European Union  
**Audience:** 1LoD operational management and Risk Committee  
**Language:** English (UK)  
**Evidence cut-off:** 15 September 2026

## Evidence scope and decision use

This evidence pack supports an EU Transport & Storage cyber-risk assessment. It distinguishes **observed activity**, **assessed exposure**, and **plausible stress scenarios**. It does not determine the legal scope, control maturity, loss exposure, insurance recovery, or supervisory status of an individual entity.

The primary evidence base comprises 2026 EU institutional and Commission publications, followed by H2 2025 material, then H1 2025 material. Older sources appear only where they provide governing legal background, transparent scenario methodology, or a completed transport case with enduring relevance.

## Source-tier assessment

| Tier | Assessment standard | Use in this pack |

## Evidence-led findings

**Observed activity.** The record supports five priority attack patterns: disruption of shared passenger-processing services, DDoS against public rail information and booking channels, credential-harvesting phishing aimed at freight workflows, state-linked multi-vector targeting across connected logistics, and potential compromise through supplier access or OT and IT interfaces. The first four have observed-activity support. The fifth is an assessed exposure derived from ENISA's sector assessment, not a named incident.

**Operational vulnerability.** EU Transport & Storage depends on integrated passenger, freight, ticketing, booking, operational-technology, information-technology and supplier environments. ENISA's sector findings indicate that older OT and IT estates, multi-party operating models, incomplete asset and dependency mapping, third-party access and uneven response testing can constrain containment and restoration. These observations do not describe a particular organisation's control environment.

**Systemic and concentration risk.** A common provider or shared sector platform can transmit disruption across airport, airline, rail, logistics or multimodal participants. The Brussels Airport event is direct evidence of a provider-linked service interruption at one EU hub. Cyber Europe 2026 provides a structured cross-border stress pathway only. It does not evidence an observed coordinated rail and maritime event.

**Material financial and regulatory impact.** The public evidence supports cost pathways, not an EU sector loss estimate: lost or delayed revenue, restoration expenditure, manual-workaround cost, customer and counterparty claims, data-related obligations, and delayed insurance recovery. Maersk's historical issuer disclosure provides contextual evidence of cost categories but is not used to estimate current EU losses. Regulatory consequences require entity scope, incident facts and national-law analysis.

## Stated limitations and data-quality constraints

No public, comprehensive EU Transport & Storage dataset links cyber events to final restoration cost, customer claims, insurance proceeds, recovery duration, liquidity effects and legal outcomes. Incident reporting is incomplete, ENISA's threat corpus is curated, and NIS360 provides EU-wide assessment rather than entity findings. Public evidence does not provide a complete Member-State NIS2 transposition map, a consolidated CER entity list, or a current inventory of competent authorities.

No cited source establishes that a named warehouse, storage or logistics firm is in NIS2 scope or has been identified under CER. A firm-level analysis requires its legal entities, functions, size, locations, digital-service role, national implementation status, notification record, supplier contracts, manual-workaround capacity, recovery objectives, cash profile and current insurance wording.
